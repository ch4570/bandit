**One feasible next test: counterbalanced tabletop location matching**

Test this narrow hypothesis: *Plain-language location descriptions improve unfamiliar volunteers’ independent shelf choices compared with current names.*

Use the six opted-in adults; record actual attendance by familiarity group. No additional recruitment. Within the two combined preparation hours, Mira and Dev prepare six retired-prop tasks, split into two roughly comparable sets of three, using the current shelf list. Prepare:

- A baseline sheet retaining current location names.
- An alternative adding plain-language equivalents.
- Identical tabletop destination cards and a fixed answer key for both conditions.

Ground descriptions and correct destinations in the supplied shelf information; do not guess ambiguous mappings. If six defensible mappings cannot be prepared, treat the exercise as blocked rather than measuring invented correctness. Keep typography, destination positions, and other information constant.

Allocate two minutes for consent and instructions, then three minutes per attendee: six shelf-choice attempts capped at twenty seconds each, with remaining time for changing sheets and resetting. Each person receives three props under each condition, without repeating a prop. Alternate condition order and set assignment within familiarity groups as far as attendance permits. Waiting participants should not watch completed attempts or discuss answers.

Mira facilitates without hints; Dev records correct unassisted choices, elapsed time, help requests and timeouts on anonymous sheets. Report counts over actual attempts, paired participant results, and familiarity groups separately. Use the five-minute wrap-up to ask what caused hesitation.

Use only removable tabletop materials, retired props and ordinary stationery within £20. Obtain agreement for handwritten notes; collect no identifiers or recordings. Leave production stock and permanent signs untouched and finish before rehearsal.

**Decision rule and limits**

Provisional threshold: if all three unfamiliar volunteers attend, at least two make more correct unassisted choices with the alternative, none does worse, and timing/help observations do not contradict improvement, devote the two weekends to further validating a simple naming aid. This is a practical screening rule, not statistical proof.

If baseline choices are already accurate, deprioritize naming. If both conditions perform poorly or results conflict, withhold the commitment. Fewer than three unfamiliar attendees leaves the threshold unresolved.

The exercise can reveal comprehension difficulties and a local signal of improvement. Small samples, unequal task difficulty and learning limit attribution. It cannot establish actual retrieval speed, return habits, performance-night benefits, adoption or payment. The next decision-changing observation is whether unfamiliar volunteers actually choose destinations more successfully—not whether they say they like the idea.
