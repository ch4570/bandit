**Opportunity and uncertainty**

There is evidence of a local problem, but its prevalence, consequences and commercial value remain uncertain:

- Five of eight intercepted volunteers recalled a search during their last two rehearsals; three of those five emphasized unfamiliar shelf names. Bea’s experience supports a naming problem, while Sol’s reliance on others suggests a possible confidence cost.
- Len offers a competing explanation: rushed returns leave props in the wrong place. Clear names cannot locate an item that was returned elsewhere. Jo identifies dependence on knowledgeable people, but has not measured its cost.
- The performance log records nine incidents across five of twelve nights. Only six incidents were timed: median four minutes, range one–eleven. This is neither a typical retrieval time nor evidence of performance delays. Missing timings, unknown reporting consistency and absent cause data limit interpretation.
- The poll shows expressed interest among self-selected respondents: 22/31 reported frustration and 18/31 would try a page. The 240 members are not known viewers or eligible crew; the separate answers cannot be linked. Two intercept participants overlap with the poll, so these are not independent samples to combine.

Dev’s claims about universal demand, £25/month payment and halving delays are unsupported. No price, purchasing decision or intervention has been tested. The room notice contributes no relevant evidence.

The immediate assumption is that **location interpretation is a meaningful, tractable obstacle when the recorded location is correct**. Other important assumptions—reliable returns and updates, sustained use, wider venue relevance and willingness to pay—remain open. Naming comes first because it has concrete local evidence and fits the permitted slot; return habits require observation under realistic pressure.

**One next test: a counterbalanced tabletop location exercise**

Use Tuesday’s six opted-in adults, retaining the distinction between three familiar and three unfamiliar volunteers. Spend the two combined preparation hours and at most £20 making two paper versions of the shelf list: current names and equivalent names with concrete descriptions. Verify descriptions against the room without touching production stock; exclude any location whose meaning cannot be verified.

Represent destinations with paper shelf/cupboard cards. Use the six retired props for six short “where would you look?” tasks, with a known correct destination for each. This deliberately holds correct storage constant.

Each participant completes three tasks using current names and three using clearer descriptions. Give each person different props in the two conditions to reduce answer recall. Across participants, rotate which props receive clearer descriptions and alternate condition order, spreading orders across familiarity groups as attendance permits. Keep layout and instructions identical.

Allow three minutes for consent and explanation, three five-minute waves with two participants working independently, and two minutes’ buffer. Mira and Dev each observe one participant. Use the additional five minutes for wrap-up. Stop before rehearsal.

With agreement, record anonymous participant codes, familiarity, first destination chosen, correctness without help, elapsed time and requests for clarification. Ask briefly what caused uncertainty. Use handwriting only; no internet, software, recordings or production changes.

**Decision rule and limits**

Treat accuracy without help as primary; time is secondary. Before the session, adopt this directional rule: devote the next two weekends to further naming research if at least two unfamiliar volunteers attend, at least two improve under clearer descriptions, none worsen, and their explanations support better understanding. Inspect task and order differences before attributing improvement to wording.

If current names already work, or clearer descriptions do not help, deprioritize naming and make return habits the next research priority. Low attendance, ambiguous destinations or contradictory results mean insufficient evidence—not success or failure.

This exercise can reveal a local comprehension mechanism and wording problems. It cannot establish real search-time savings, rushed return behavior, performance-night benefits, adoption, payment or general demand. Even a positive result supports further research, not building or selling Backstage Bin.
