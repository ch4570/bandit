**Opportunity and uncertainty**

There is a credible local problem, but its scale and causes remain uncertain:

- Five of eight intercepted volunteers recalled searching during their last two rehearsals; three of those five emphasized unfamiliar shelf names. Bea’s account supports a naming problem. Len’s account suggests a competing cause: props are returned to convenient, incorrect places. Sol and Jo suggest dependence on knowledgeable people, with possible embarrassment and interruption costs.
- The poll shows interest among self-selected respondents: 22 of 31 reported frustration and 18 would try a location page. Unknown views, mixed membership, and absent cross-tabulation prevent conclusions about crew-wide demand or whether frustrated respondents wanted the page. Two intercept participants also answered the poll, so these are not fully independent samples.
- Nine incidents across five of twelve performance nights suggest recurring searches. The four-minute median applies only to six timed incidents. Missing timings, unknown recording consistency, and no retrieval denominator prevent reliable estimates of frequency or total burden. Searches were not recorded as performance delays.

Dev’s claims exceed this evidence. Nobody has evaluated a price or offered payment; the poll does not establish demand across theatres, and nothing supports halving delays.

The critical assumptions are that naming confusion contributes meaningfully to searches; clearer descriptions reduce that confusion; and recorded locations remain accurate despite rushed returns. Whether crews would maintain a system, whether it improves live operations, and whether anyone would pay remain separate uncertainties. Naming is the best next target because the permitted exercise can isolate it; this slot cannot credibly reproduce rushed return habits.

**One next test: counterbalanced tabletop location interpretation**

Test the hypothesis: *plain-language descriptions improve correct first-choice destination identification compared with current shelf names.*

Within the two shared preparation hours, select six retired props with unambiguous destinations in the current shelf list. Prepare tabletop destination cards representing those locations, using recognizable physical descriptions. Prepare two versions of each prop’s lookup entry: its current shelf name, and that name plus a clear physical description. Check the intended destinations against available venue information; exclude ambiguous items rather than invent answers. Use identical destination cards and layout in both conditions. Spend no more than £20 on printing and stationery.

Use two complementary task sheets. Each participant gets three current-name tasks and three clarified-name tasks, with no prop repeated. Across sheets, swap which props receive clarification and vary task order. Distribute sheets as evenly as possible within familiar and unfamiliar participants. This reduces item and order effects without teaching the same answer twice.

Allow two minutes for the introduction and agreement to anonymous handwritten notes, then six individual three-minute turns. One team member facilitates; the other records. Participants use the list to choose a destination card for each prop, without coaching or watching earlier turns. Explain that this tests the information, not their competence.

Record familiarity category, condition, correct first choice, and requests for help; use completion time only as supporting evidence. Finish with the permitted five-minute wrap-up, asking which wording caused uncertainty. Keep production stock and permanent signs untouched; use no internet, software, recordings, or personal identifiers.

**Decision rule and limits**

With all six attending, each condition yields nine newcomer choices and nine familiar-volunteer choices. Treat at least three fewer incorrect newcomer choices under clarification, with no increase among familiar volunteers, as a practical signal to devote the next two weekends to investigating location naming and real-world transfer—not to building an app. This is a decision threshold, not statistical proof.

Little improvement despite baseline errors would shift priority toward location accuracy and return habits. Near-perfect baseline performance, conflicting results, or low attendance would be inconclusive; do not force a verdict or commit both weekends.

The exercise can establish whether wording helps these volunteers interpret these tabletop destinations. It cannot establish faster physical searches, sustained correct returns, performance-night benefits, adoption elsewhere, or willingness to pay.
