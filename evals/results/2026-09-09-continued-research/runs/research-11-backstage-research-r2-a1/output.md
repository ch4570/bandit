**Recommendation: use the afternoon to test whether clearer location names help unfamiliar volunteers choose the correct prop destination.** This is a proposed research step, not approval to build. All evidence in the packet is synthetic; it supports reasoning within this scenario, not claims about real customers.

The opportunity is plausible but narrow: volunteers retrieving or returning props may struggle with local naming conventions and depend on experienced colleagues. Backstage Bin could reduce that dependence. Whether location clarity solves enough of the problem to merit two weekends remains uncertain.

The most consequential assumptions are that confusing names materially contribute to errors; that locations remain accurate despite rushed returns; and that someone would maintain and pay for the aid. The user is a volunteer; the buyer and purchasing authority remain unknown. Existing alternatives include the shelf list and asking experienced people. Naming is the best immediate uncertainty to test because it has specific supporting accounts and fits the permitted exercise. Return habits under rehearsal pressure do not.

**One feasible test: a counterbalanced tabletop location exercise**

Use Tuesday’s existing opt-in slot with whoever attends from the three familiar and three unfamiliar adult volunteers. No additional recruitment.

Within the two combined preparation hours and £20 limit, prepare six retired props, paper destination zones representing shelf-list locations, and two list versions: current names and the same names with plain-language explanatory inserts. Keep the destination layout and prop assignments identical across versions. Make any illustrative location descriptions explicit; unverified descriptions cannot establish accuracy in the real room.

Each participant completes six individual destination-choice tasks: three using each version, with different props in each condition. Across participants, rotate which props receive each version and alternate version order within experience groups as evenly as attendance allows. Keep waiting participants from watching. This reduces, without eliminating, learning and prop-difficulty effects.

Allow two minutes for instructions and agreement to anonymous handwritten notes, up to fifteen minutes for six individual turns, and three minutes of buffer; retain the permitted five-minute wrap-up. Cap each choice at fifteen seconds. Mira presents standardized prompts without coaching; Dev records condition, experience group, correctness, help requests and timeouts. Use temporary task numbers only, with no personal identifiers, photographs or recordings. Keep production stock and permanent signs untouched; stop before rehearsal.

**Interpretation and decision rule**

Primary measure: correct, unaided choices divided by completed opportunities in each condition, reported separately by experience group. Show individual paired counts; record missed tasks separately from errors. A timeout is an unsuccessful exposed task.

A **provisional learning threshold**, not statistical proof: at least two unfamiliar volunteers complete both conditions and each improves by at least one correct choice out of three, without a repeated new ambiguity. That would justify spending the next two weekends exploring a simple location aid and its upkeep, without committing to an app.

If unfamiliar volunteers struggle equally under both versions, deprioritize naming and reconsider return accuracy or other causes. If both versions work well, attendance is insufficient, or results depend on order or one prop, treat the exercise as inconclusive and withhold the build commitment.

This test could establish whether explanatory names aid tabletop interpretation here. It cannot establish reliable real-world returns, halved delays, adoption, payment or benefits elsewhere. **No test has been conducted; outcomes remain unobserved.**
