**Recommendation: use the agreed slot to test whether clearer location descriptions help unfamiliar volunteers identify a prop’s intended location without asking someone.** This is a plausible, cheaply testable part of Backstage Bin’s opportunity. It does not yet justify an app or a £25 monthly offer.

All evidence below is synthetic and packet-only. The proposed test has not been conducted.

**What the evidence supports**

- **A local problem worth investigating:** five of eight intercepted Lantern Hall volunteers recalled searching during their last two rehearsals; three of those five emphasized unfamiliar shelf names. Bea’s account suggests that translating local terminology could help newcomers. Sol currently asks nearby people, while Jo describes dependence on whoever remembers the location. These are reported experiences, not measured observations.
- **Recorded searches, with uncertain consequences:** the venue log records nine incidents across five of twelve performance nights. Only six incidents were timed: one to eleven minutes, median four. Causes, retrieval volume and actual performance delays are unknown; incomplete recording prevents estimating an incident rate per retrieval or total time lost.
- **Interest, not demand:** 22 of 31 voluntary poll respondents selected frustration, and 18 selected willingness to try a page. Unknown views, mixed crew/non-crew membership and self-selection prevent population estimates. The separate questions cannot establish that frustrated respondents wanted the page. Two intercept participants also answered the poll; the samples cannot be treated as independent.
- **A competing explanation:** Len attributes misplaced props to hurried returns. Clear descriptions could help people understand a location while leaving the prop absent from it.

Dev’s claims about universal willingness to pay £25 monthly and halving delays are unsupported. There were no prices presented, purchasing conversations or outcome trials. The accidental room notice contributes no relevant evidence.

**The assumptions that matter**

The immediate uncertainty is whether unfamiliar naming creates an independently solvable obstacle. The next is whether listed locations remain accurate despite return habits; a useful location aid depends on both. Longer term, the buyer, willingness to pay, upkeep responsibility and repeated use remain unknown.

Start provisionally with unfamiliar or occasional Lantern Hall helpers. Their current alternatives are the shelf list, asking experienced volunteers and learning local terminology. A paper insert might already deliver the proposed benefit. Location naming is the best first test because the permitted tabletop setting can isolate comprehension; it cannot credibly reproduce rushed production returns.

**One next test: a counterbalanced tabletop location exercise**

**Question:** With the intended location held accurate, does adding a plain-language description reduce unfamiliar volunteers’ wrong choices and requests for help?

Use only the six opted-in adults who attend next Tuesday. No additional recruitment, software or internet is needed.

Mira and Dev would use their combined two preparation hours to make a small tabletop representation of shelf destinations, using the current shelf list, six retired props and removable cards. Prepare two otherwise identical list versions: current names alone, and current names plus plain-language descriptions tied to visible tabletop cues. Do not invent claims about actual room geography. Establish an answer key and check that each description identifies exactly one model destination.

Create two matched sets of three prop-location choices, using different destinations where possible to limit learning. Each participant completes one set under each list version, choosing the intended destination for each prop. Alternate version order within familiarity groups and rotate which set receives the added descriptions. Keep layout, print size, instructions and task limits constant.

Use two minutes for consent and instructions, then six individual two-minute turns, leaving six minutes for resets or overruns within the twenty-minute slot. Prevent participants watching preceding turns; otherwise record that exposure as a limitation. Allow up to forty-five seconds per set. Mira facilitates neutrally; Dev records. The separate five-minute wrap-up covers brief explanations of confusing terms.

With agreement, record anonymous trial codes, familiarity group, condition order, correct choices out of three, help requests and unfinished choices. Report people and task counts separately; six people’s repeated choices are not independent participants. Compare unfamiliar volunteers primarily and familiar volunteers as context. Exclude names, photographs and recordings.

Keep printing and stationery within £20. Use only retired props; leave production stock and permanent signs untouched and stop before rehearsal.

**How the result changes the decision**

These are provisional decision rules, not statistical proof:

- **Proceed narrowly:** if all three unfamiliar volunteers attend, at least two improve by at least one correct unaided choice, and none worsens, devote the next two weekends to exploring a simple location aid and its real-workflow usefulness—not automatically an app.
- **Redirect:** if unfamiliar volunteers struggle under both versions, do not commit to the naming solution. Their observed errors should determine whether descriptions, the model or another obstacle deserves attention.
- **Deprioritize naming:** if they succeed unaided with current names, the exercise supplies little reason to invest two weekends in clearer naming.
- **Inconclusive:** fewer than three unfamiliar attendees, mixed results, coaching, substantial learning effects or an ambiguous answer key should not trigger a build commitment.

The test could establish a local comprehension signal under controlled conditions. It cannot establish accurate returns, performance-night savings, adoption, payment or transfer to other venues. The decision-changing observation is whether unfamiliar people make better unaided location choices when only the descriptions change.
