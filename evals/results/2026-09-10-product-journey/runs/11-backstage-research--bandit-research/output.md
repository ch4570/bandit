**Assumptions that matter most**

1. **Location interpretation is a meaningful source of difficulty.** Several accounts support this, but no recorded incidents identify their cause. This is the best uncertainty to reduce in the permitted tabletop setting.
2. **Assigned locations reflect actual prop locations.** Len’s account challenges this. A location aid loses value when returns are inconsistent.
3. **An aid remains useful enough to maintain and pay for.** Repeat use, ownership, willingness to pay, and delivery costs are wholly untested.

Return habits may ultimately matter more. However, a calm tabletop exercise cannot credibly reproduce rushed rehearsal returns; wording comprehension is more directly observable within this slot.

**One proposed test: paired shelf-location interpretation**

**Question:** Can plain-language explanations of current shelf names reduce unaided interpretation errors among unfamiliar volunteers?

Use the existing Tuesday slot and six opted-in adults only. Attendance remains uncertain. The plan is unexecuted; no outcomes exist.

Within the **two combined preparation hours and £20 stationery limit**, Mira prepares six paper destination cards representing locations on the current shelf list, with physical descriptions and a fixed answer key. Dev prepares two versions of the list: current wording and the same wording with short, concrete explanations. Keep typography, layout, and other information identical. Check that descriptions accurately represent the intended locations; ambiguous answer keys would invalidate the exercise.

Use the six retired props, each linked to an assigned destination in the copied list. Participants place each prop beside the destination card they believe matches its listed location. This tests interpretation, not walking through or searching the real room.

Each participant completes six brief tasks: three using current wording and three using explained wording, with different destinations across conditions. Alternate which version comes first, and reverse the allocation of destinations across participants as evenly as attendance allows, including within familiarity groups. Reset between participants; prevent watching earlier attempts and defer corrective feedback until completion.

Allocate three minutes for instructions and agreement to anonymous handwritten notes, approximately fifteen minutes for six individual turns, and two minutes of buffer. Use the additional five minutes for wrap-up. Mira facilitates neutrally; Dev records. Stop at rehearsal time. Use no internet, software, production stock, permanent signs, photographs, recordings, or personal identifiers.

**Measure:** correct destination choices without help within twenty seconds, reported as counts out of three tasks per condition for each participant. Record wrong choices, requests for help, and timeouts separately. Report familiar and unfamiliar groups separately, alongside actual attendance and incomplete tasks; repeated tasks are not independent people.

**Decision rule and limits**

The following is a proposed practical threshold, not a statistical validation standard:

- **Proceed narrowly:** at least two unfamiliar attendees improve by at least one correct task and achieve at least two of three correct with explanations. This would justify devoting the weekends to further validation of a simple location aid, without committing to an app.
- **Deprioritize wording:** unfamiliar attendees already succeed on all current-wording tasks, or explanations produce no consistent improvement. Do not commit both weekends to this approach; reconsider the unresolved return-location problem.
- **Inconclusive:** fewer than two unfamiliar attendees complete both conditions, unclear answer keys, coaching, substantial task imbalance, or mixed results. Make no development commitment from that result.

Success would support comprehensibility of these descriptions in this exercise. It would not establish faster real searches, fewer misplaced props, performance-night benefits, adoption elsewhere, repeat use, or payment. The decision-changing observation is whether unfamiliar volunteers actually make more correct unaided choices—not whether they say they like the idea.
