# Research a decision

Start from the decision the research should change. Reuse available evidence;
do not require a new market study before every reversible product draft.

## Establish the business question

Identify the relevant actor, recurring situation, current workaround, and the
cost of that workaround. Separate user, buyer, and approver when their incentives
differ. For an early app, a useful provisional thesis connects:

- Who has the problem and what triggers it?
- Why would they switch from the current alternative?
- How can the team reach them initially?
- Who pays, for what unit of value, with what meaningful cost assumptions?
- Which uncertain claim would make the chosen approach fail?

Investigate only questions needed for this decision. Label a recommended segment,
price, or channel as a hypothesis if the user has delegated the draft without data.
Avoid replacing the chosen idea with a broad ideation exercise.

## Collect evidence that can bear the claim

For interviews, separate observed/reported past behavior from opinions about a
future product. Preserve disconfirming accounts and recruitment context. A small
convenience sample can suggest a problem, but does not establish its prevalence.
Synthetic personas and interview examples must be labeled as synthetic.

For competitive or market research, use direct product documentation, current
pricing, filings, original studies, or first-party data when available. Record
date and plan/region where relevant. A competitor's claim is attributable
positioning, not independently verified customer success. If browsing is
unavailable, say which claims remain unverified and give a useful desk-research
plan rather than fabricating links.

Use market sizing only when it helps choose. A bottom-up estimate should expose
reachable population, eligibility, conversion assumption, unit, period, and price.
Distinguish total market from the customers this team can reach. Show a range when
reasonable assumptions change the conclusion; avoid ornamental precision.

## Choose the next informative experiment

Connect the uncertain claim to an observable behavior. State the audience and
recruitment path, offer/prototype conditions, exposure opportunity and window,
measure and denominator, resource constraint, and what each plausible result
would change. A target is a proposed decision threshold, not an observation or a
universal product-market-fit cutoff. Choose its level using context and the cost
of a wrong decision; leave it provisional when that context is missing.

Match the evidence to its meaning:

| Observation | Can inform | Does not by itself establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interviewed about a past problem | Situation, workaround, language | Population-wide demand |
| Joined a free waitlist | Interest under that offer | Willingness to pay |
| Paid deposit/preorder | Commitment at stated conditions | Retention, profitable delivery, or unrefunded revenue |
| Received value in a pilot | Feasibility/value in that setting | Scalable acquisition or recurring use |
| Paid and used again | Repeat behavior over the observed window | Retention beyond that window |

A lightweight prototype or concierge test can answer a question without building
the full app. It still needs a relevant audience and a way to observe the behavior.
Suggest outreach or launch materials when requested; research does not itself
authorize sending them or running a public experiment.

## Interpret results before recommending a decision

Read the actual recruitment, eligibility, exposures, time window, denominator,
missing events, refunds, and guardrails. Separate people with no opportunity to
act yet from those who had an opportunity and did not act. State calculation
inputs and keep counts alongside rates, especially for small samples.

If pricing, onboarding, audience, or features changed together, describe the
observed combined change. Do not attribute the difference to a single factor
without a design that supports that inference. For an A/B experiment, check
assignment and exposure integrity, sample-ratio imbalance, duration, repeated
peeking, and intended guardrails before relying on a lift claim. Do not imply
statistical certainty from an arbitrary percentage improvement.

Classify the relevant hypothesis as supported within scope, contradicted within
scope, or still unresolved. Recommend proceed, change, narrow, or stop with the
evidence and consequence of being wrong. Report conflicting signals and the next
least expensive observation that could reverse the recommendation.
